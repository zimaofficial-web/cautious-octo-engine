# Research & Information Processing Guidelines

> [!NOTE]
> **Context Isolation:** This guide outlines investigative research, source verification, and analytical synthesis. It enforces rigorous intellectual standards without software engineering constraints.

---

## 1. Research Methodology & Systematic Inquiry

### 1.1 Formulating the Research Question
A strong research question must be:
- **Focused:** Specific enough to be addressed comprehensively within scope.
- **Analytical, Not Descriptive:** Demands evaluation and evidence rather than a simple dictionary definition (ask *How*, *Why*, or *To what extent*, rather than *What is*).
- **Falsifiable / Empirically Grounded:** Supported by measurable data, historical precedent, or observable findings.

### 1.2 Primary vs. Secondary Sources
- **Primary Sources (Gold Standard):** Original research papers, raw datasets, official government publications, legal statutes, patent filings, firsthand interviews, or company financial filings (10-K).
- **Secondary Sources:** Industry analysis reports, review articles, trade journalism, and critical commentaries analyzing primary work.
- **Rule of Proximity:** Always trace citations back to the primary source. Never cite a blog post's summary of a study without inspecting the original methodology.

---

## 2. Source Evaluation: The CRAAP + Lateral Reading Protocol

Evaluate every source across 5 rigorous dimensions:
1. **Currency:** When was this information published or last updated? In fast-moving disciplines (AI, medicine, tech), sources older than 18–24 months may contain superseded claims.
2. **Relevance:** Does the data directly answer your specific research question, or is it tangential background noise?
3. **Authority:** Who is the author or publisher? What are their credentials, institutional affiliations, and publication history? Have they been peer-reviewed?
4. **Accuracy:** Are assertions backed by verifiable evidence, mathematical proof, or replicable data? Are methodology limitations explicitly acknowledged?
5. **Purpose / Bias:** What is the intent of the publication? Is it academic inquiry, commercial marketing, political lobbying, or sponsored research? Check funding declarations and conflicts of interest.

### The Lateral Reading Technique
When encountering an unfamiliar source or publication:
- Do **not** stay on their "About Us" page.
- Open separate tabs to research what independent external scholars, industry peers, and watchdog organizations say about that author or institution.

---

## 3. Fact-Checking & Claims Classification

When synthesizing any complex body of text, classify every assertion into one of four buckets:
- **[VERIFIED FACT]:** Backed by peer-reviewed consensus, official records, or directly measurable empirical data.
- **[PLAUSIBLE ESTIMATE / PROJECTION]:** Based on sound statistical modeling, but subject to assumptions and future variables.
- **[OPINION / PERSPECTIVE]:** Subjective judgment, philosophical argument, or value-based assertion. Must be attributed directly to the speaker ("According to Dr. Smith...").
- **[UNSUBSTANTIATED / MARKETING HYPE]:** Vague claims ("industry-leading", "proven to revolutionize") devoid of comparative data or testable metrics. Flag or discard.

## 4. Synthesis & Document Analysis Frameworks

### 4.1 The Synthesis Matrix (Comparative Analysis)
When reviewing multiple papers or reports on the same topic, organize findings in an evidence matrix:

| Source / Author (Year) | Core Methodology | Sample Size / Dataset | Key Findings | Stated Limitations | Contradictions with Other Sources |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Paper A (2025) | Randomized Controlled Trial | $N=1,200$ users | +18% task efficiency | Limited to desktop users | Disagrees with Paper B on latency impact |
| Paper B (2026) | Observational Cohort | $N=4,500$ users | No significant gain | Retrospective data only | Relied on self-reported survey answers |

### 4.2 Handling Contradictions & Outliers
When reputable sources disagree:
- Do not arbitrarily choose the one that confirms your bias.
- Explain the divergent conditions: examine differences in sample population, geographic constraints, measurement criteria, or operational definitions.

---

## 5. Citation Standards & Academic Integrity

Always cite sources consistently:
- **APA 7th (Social sciences, business, psychology):**
  - In-text: `(Vaswani et al., 2017)`
  - Reference: `Vaswani, A., Shazeer, N., Parmar, N., et al. (2017). Attention is all you need. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems, 30.`
- **IEEE (Computer science, electrical engineering):**
  - In-text: `[1]`
  - Reference: `[1] A. Vaswani et al., "Attention is all you need," in Adv. Neural Inf. Process. Syst., vol. 30, 2017.`
- **Chicago Notes & Bibliography (Humanities, history, arts):**
  - Footnotes paired with a comprehensive formal bibliography.
